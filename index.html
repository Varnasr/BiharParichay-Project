Overview

An introduction to one of India's most historically significant and complex states

Bihar, located in eastern India, is the third most populous state in the country with an estimated population exceeding 128 million (Registrar General of India, 2011 Census projected to 2024). It occupies 94,163 square kilometres of the eastern Gangetic plain, bounded by Nepal to the north, Jharkhand to the south, West Bengal to the east, and Uttar Pradesh to the west.

The state's significance extends far beyond its demographic weight. Bihar is the historical heartland of some of the world's oldest civilisations and philosophical traditions. The Maurya Empire (322-185 BCE), one of the largest in antiquity, was administered from Pataliputra (modern Patna). The ancient university of Nalanda, a UNESCO World Heritage Site, drew scholars from across Asia for over seven centuries. Buddhism and Jainism both trace foundational moments to this region -- Siddhartha Gautama attained enlightenment at Bodh Gaya, and Mahavira, the 24th Tirthankara, was born in Vaishali.

Contemporary Bihar presents a study in contrasts. Despite its rich historical legacy, the state ranks among India's least developed on multiple indicators: per capita income, urbanisation rates, and human development indices. Yet it has also demonstrated periods of rapid economic growth -- averaging over 10% GSDP growth between 2005 and 2015 -- and has made notable strides in road infrastructure, law enforcement, and poverty reduction. Understanding Bihar requires engaging with its layered history, complex social structure, economic challenges, and cultural richness simultaneously.

Geography & Maps

The eastern Gangetic plain -- rivers, terrain, and administrative divisions

Bihar lies entirely within the middle and lower Gangetic plain, one of the most fertile alluvial regions in the world. The Ganga river bisects the state roughly east-west, creating a natural division between the flood-prone northern plains (Mithila, Tirhut, and Kosi regions) and the relatively elevated southern plateau fringes. The state's geography is dominated by its river systems: the Ganga, Gandak, Budhi Gandak, Bagmati, Kamla, Kosi, and Son rivers shape both the physical landscape and the livelihoods of millions.

Northern Bihar is particularly vulnerable to seasonal flooding. The Kosi river, historically known as the "Sorrow of Bihar," has shifted its course by over 150 km westward in the past 200 years, creating one of the largest inland deltas in the world. The 2008 Kosi floods displaced over 3 million people and affected 16 districts.

Demographics & Society

Population, caste structure, religion, literacy, and urbanisation patterns

Bihar's demographic profile is defined by high population density, a young age structure, low urbanisation, and a deeply stratified social hierarchy. With a population of 104.1 million (Census 2011) and a decadal growth rate of 25.4% (2001-2011), Bihar is India's third most populous state. The population density of 1,106 persons per square kilometre is the highest in India, placing immense pressure on land, water, and public services.

The state's social structure is profoundly shaped by caste. The caste system in Bihar is among the most entrenched in India, influencing electoral politics, access to education, land ownership patterns, and social mobility. According to Census 2011 and NFHS-5 data, Scheduled Castes constitute approximately 15.9% of the population, Scheduled Tribes 1.3%, and Other Backward Classes (OBCs) represent a substantial majority. The Mandal Commission (1980) and subsequent Bihar-specific backward class commissions have shaped reservation policies that remain central to state politics.

Literacy, while improving, remains a challenge. The overall literacy rate stood at 63.82% in 2011 (male: 73.39%, female: 53.33%), a significant improvement from 47% in 2001 but still below the national average of 74.04%. The gender gap in literacy (20 percentage points) reflects broader patterns of gender inequality in the state.

Economy & Development

GSDP trends, sectoral composition, agriculture, and migration economy

Bihar's economy presents a paradox of rapid growth from a low base. Between 2005-06 and 2014-15, the state's Gross State Domestic Product (GSDP) grew at an average of 10.5% annually -- among the fastest in India -- driven by improvements in governance, road construction, and public investment. However, Bihar's per capita Net State Domestic Product (NSDP) remains among the lowest nationally: Rs 54,383 in 2021-22 (at current prices), compared to the all-India average of Rs 1,50,726 (RBI Handbook of Statistics on Indian States, 2023).

The economy remains heavily service-oriented by contribution to GSDP, but agriculture continues to employ the majority of the workforce. Over 76% of Bihar's population depends on agriculture and allied activities for livelihood, though agriculture contributes only about 18-20% of GSDP. Key crops include rice (kharif), wheat (rabi), maize, lentils (masoor, arhar), and sugarcane. Bihar is India's largest producer of litchi and makhana (fox nuts), and a significant producer of vegetables.

The remittance economy is a critical but often underappreciated dimension. Bihar is one of India's largest source states for internal migration. Millions of Bihari workers migrate to states like Maharashtra, Gujarat, Delhi, Punjab, and Kerala for employment. Remittances are estimated to contribute significantly to household income, particularly in districts like Darbhanga, Madhubani, Siwan, and Gopalganj (Deshingkar & Akter, 2009; Keshri & Bhagat, 2012).

Social Issues & Challenges

Poverty, floods, health, education gaps, gender, and migration

Poverty & Inequality. Bihar has the highest incidence of multidimensional poverty in India. According to the NITI Aayog's National Multidimensional Poverty Index (2021), 51.91% of Bihar's population was multidimensionally poor -- the highest share among all states. While this represents significant improvement from 77.4% in 2005-06, the absolute numbers remain staggering. Poverty is concentrated in rural areas and disproportionately affects SC/ST and EBC households.

Flooding. North Bihar is among India's most flood-prone regions. The Kosi, Gandak, Bagmati, Kamla, and Burhi Gandak rivers, originating in Nepal, carry enormous sediment loads that elevate riverbeds over time. An estimated 73.06% of the North Bihar plain is flood-affected (Bihar State Disaster Management Authority). The 2008 Kosi embankment breach displaced 3.3 million people across 993 villages. Annual monsoon flooding continues to affect 15-20 million people, destroy crops, and damage infrastructure.

Health. Key health indicators from NFHS-5 (2019-21) show Bihar lagging behind national averages: the Infant Mortality Rate stands at 46.8 per 1,000 live births (India: 35.2), under-5 stunting at 42.9% (India: 35.5%), and full immunisation coverage at 75.9% (India: 76.4%). The Total Fertility Rate of 3.0 remains the highest in India, contributing to rapid population growth. The institutional delivery rate has improved significantly to 74.4% but gaps persist in rural areas.

Education. Despite improvements in enrolment driven by schemes like the mid-day meal programme and Mukhyamantri Cycle Yojana (providing bicycles to girls), quality remains a concern. The ASER 2022 report found that only 27.8% of Class V students in Bihar could read a Class II level text, and learning outcomes consistently rank among the lowest nationally. Teacher absenteeism and vacant posts (estimated at 1.5-2 lakh unfilled positions) compound these challenges.

Gender. Bihar has one of India's highest rates of child marriage. NFHS-5 reports that 40.8% of women aged 20-24 were married before age 18 (India: 23.3%). Women's workforce participation is low -- only 9.8% of women aged 15-49 are employed (NFHS-5). These indicators reflect deeply rooted patriarchal norms that limit women's mobility, education, and economic agency.

Notable People & Leadership

Historical figures, intellectual legacy, and contributions to India's democratic fabric

Bihar's contribution to Indian civilisation and democratic governance is disproportionately large relative to its contemporary economic standing. The region has been the birthplace or formative ground of emperors, philosophers, mathematicians, and political leaders whose influence extends across centuries and borders.

Chandragupta Maurya (c. 340-298 BCE)

Founder of the Maurya Empire, one of the largest empires of antiquity, administered from Pataliputra (Patna). United most of the Indian subcontinent under a single administration for the first time.

Ashoka the Great (c. 304-232 BCE)

Mauryan emperor who, after the Kalinga War, embraced Buddhism and propagated principles of non-violence and dharma through edicts across the subcontinent. Widely regarded as one of history's most enlightened rulers.

Aryabhata (476-550 CE)

Mathematician and astronomer, likely born in Kusumapura (Patna). His work Aryabhatiya introduced the concept of zero as a placeholder, calculated pi to four decimal places, and proposed a heliocentric model of the solar system.

Guru Gobind Singh (1666-1708)

The tenth Sikh Guru was born at Patna Sahib. He founded the Khalsa in 1699 and is revered for his contributions to Sikh theology, warrior tradition, and literary works.

Dr. Rajendra Prasad (1884-1963)

First President of independent India (1950-1962). Born in Ziradei, Siwan district. A leading figure in the Indian independence movement and President of the Constituent Assembly that drafted India's Constitution.

Jayaprakash Narayan (1902-1979)

Known as "Loknayak" (People's Hero). Led the Total Revolution (Sampoorna Kranti) movement in 1974 that contributed to the fall of the Indira Gandhi government. Born in Sitab Diara, Saran district. A pivotal figure in Indian democratic history.

Anugrah Narayan Sinha (1887-1957)

Bihar's first Deputy Chief Minister and Finance Minister. Known as "Bihar Vibhuti." Led the Champaran Satyagraha alongside Gandhi and was central to Bihar's land reform legislation.

Vidyapati Thakur (c. 1352-1448)

Maithili poet, considered one of the greatest lyric poets of medieval India. His works on love and devotion profoundly influenced Bengali, Assamese, and Odia literary traditions, and he is a foundational figure in Maithili cultural identity.

Food & Cuisine

Regional culinary traditions, staple foods, and the agriculture-cuisine nexus

Bihar's culinary traditions are deeply rooted in its agrarian economy, river systems, and cultural diversity. The cuisine is characterised by its reliance on sattu (roasted gram flour), rice, wheat, dal (lentils), and seasonal vegetables, with distinct regional variations across the Mithila, Magadh, and Bhojpur cultural zones. Unlike the restaurant-driven visibility of Punjabi or South Indian cuisine, Bihari food traditions remain largely home-cooked and tied to ritual, season, and locality.

Litti Chokha

Bihar's most iconic dish. Litti are balls of whole wheat dough stuffed with sattu mixed with ajwain, mustard oil, and spices, baked over cow-dung cakes or coal. Chokha is a mash of roasted eggplant, tomato, and potato. A complete, protein-rich meal with ancient origins -- likely predating the tandoor tradition.

Sattu Preparations

Sattu (roasted gram flour) is Bihar's superfood. It is consumed as sattu paratha (stuffed flatbread), sattu sharbat (a cooling drink with lemon and salt), and sattu ka laddoo. High in protein and fibre, it is the traditional working-class energy food and is gaining national recognition as a health food.

Dal Pitha / Malpua

Dal Pitha, rice flour dumplings stuffed with spiced lentils and steamed, is a staple of Mithila cuisine. Malpua, a deep-fried sweet pancake soaked in sugar syrup, is prepared during Holi and Chhath Puja. Both reflect the rice-based culinary traditions of northern Bihar.

Thekua

A sweet, crunchy preparation of wheat flour, jaggery, and ghee, Thekua is the quintessential Chhath Puja offering. It is also known as Khajuria in some regions. The dish holds deep religious and cultural significance and is prepared in nearly every household during the festival.

Makhana (Fox Nuts)

Bihar produces over 90% of India's makhana, harvested from ponds and wetlands primarily in Mithilanchal districts (Darbhanga, Madhubani, Saharsa). Eaten roasted as a snack, in curries, and in kheer. Increasingly exported as a premium health food. The traditional harvesting process involves wading in chest-deep water -- physically demanding labour predominantly performed by Mallah communities.

Khaja & Tilkut

Khaja, layered flaky pastry soaked in sugar syrup, is associated with Rajgir and Silao (GI-tagged "Silao Khaja"). Tilkut, made from pounded sesame seeds and sugar, is a Gaya speciality consumed during Makar Sankranti. Both represent Bihar's significant tradition of festival confectionery.

Art, Culture & Heritage

Visual arts, performing traditions, UNESCO heritage sites, languages, and festivals

Bihar's cultural heritage spans millennia, encompassing visual arts, literary traditions, architectural monuments, and living folk practices. The region's cultural identity is shaped by multiple linguistic communities -- Maithili (a constitutionally recognised language under the Eighth Schedule), Bhojpuri, Magahi, and Angika -- each with distinct literary canons, musical traditions, and ritual practices.

Madhubani / Mithila Painting

A centuries-old tradition from the Mithila region (Madhubani, Darbhanga districts), characterised by geometric patterns, bright colours, and mythological themes. Traditionally painted by women on walls and floors using natural pigments. Received a GI (Geographical Indication) tag in 2007. Internationally exhibited and collected. Sita Devi and Ganga Devi are among its most celebrated practitioners.

Manjusha Art

Originating in the Bhagalpur-Anga region, Manjusha art involves painting on boxes (manjusha) used in the Bishhari Puja festival. It features distinctive serpent motifs and narrative scenes from the legend of Bihula-Bishari. One of India's few scroll-like narrative painting traditions.

Nalanda Mahavihara (UNESCO)

The archaeological remains of Nalanda, inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2016, represent one of the earliest and most important universities in the ancient world. Active from the 5th to 12th centuries CE, it hosted up to 10,000 students and 2,000 teachers from across Asia, with a library said to have burned for three months when destroyed.

Mahabodhi Temple, Bodh Gaya (UNESCO)

UNESCO World Heritage Site since 2002. Marks the spot where Siddhartha Gautama attained enlightenment under the Bodhi Tree around 528 BCE. The current temple structure dates to the 5th-6th century CE. One of four holy sites of Buddhism and a major pilgrimage destination drawing visitors from across the world.

Chhath Puja

Bihar's most significant festival, dedicated to Surya (the Sun god) and Chhathi Maiya. Observed with a 36-hour waterless fast, ritual bathing in rivers, and offerings at sunrise and sunset. The festival has spread with Bihari diaspora across India and abroad. It has been submitted for UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage recognition.

Folk Performing Arts

Bidesia: A dramatic folk form created by Bhikhari Thakur (1887-1971), the "Shakespeare of Bhojpuri," addressing themes of migration, marital separation, and social exploitation. Jat-Jatin: A Mithila folk dance depicting conjugal love during monsoon. Domkach: Wedding-associated folk singing tradition. Sonepur Mela: Asia's largest cattle fair, held at the Ganga-Gandak confluence, combining commerce, culture, and pilgrimage.

Sujini & Sikki Craft

Sujini embroidery, from Muzaffarpur and surrounding districts, involves quilting old saris with narrative threadwork. Sikki grass craft, using golden-coloured Sikki grass, produces ornamental baskets, containers, and figures -- primarily a women's craft tradition in Mithila. Both received recognition through government craft promotion programmes.

Languages & Literary Traditions

Bihar is linguistically diverse: Maithili (recognised in Constitution's 8th Schedule, 2003), Bhojpuri (widely spoken, seeking 8th Schedule status), Magahi, and Angika. Maithili literature traces back to Vidyapati (14th c.). Modern Hindi literature includes significant Bihari contributions from Ramdhari Singh Dinkar, Phanishwarnath Renu (Maila Anchal), and Nagarjun.
